Prototyping with a 3D printer

With an FDM printer you go from design to a hand-held sample in hours. Ideal for fit and function, only limited for statements about series production.

  • Free
  • No sign-up
  • No data sent: inputs stay in your browser

What printed prototypes are good for

  • Checking fit and dimensions on a real part
  • Judging ergonomics, grip and size
  • Testing housings, mounts and assembly
  • Comparing variants quickly before money goes into tooling

Honest limits

A printed prototype does not behave like an injection-moulded part. Strength along the layers, surface, accuracy and material behaviour differ. Statements about lifetime, approval or series cost can only be drawn from it to a limited extent. Check critical properties with the later manufacturing process.

Process

  1. Set the goal: what should the prototype show: dimensions, function or looks?
  2. Print fast: thick layers (0.2 to 0.3 mm) and low infill (10 to 15 %) save time and money, see save costs.
  3. Test the fit: allow clearance using the guide values.
  4. Check: verify size and position in the print bed preview.
  5. Iterate: adjust dimensions and reprint. Housings and mounts can be changed parametrically with the generators.

Material for prototypes

PLA is enough for almost every sample: cheap, accurate, easy. If a function test needs toughness or heat, use PETG or ASA. See the materials overview.

Frequently asked questions

How quickly can I get a prototype?

Small parts are often printed in one to three hours, larger ones take a day. Thick layers and low infill speed it up.

Is an FDM prototype as strong as the production part?

Usually not. Printed parts are weaker along the layers and differ in material.

Which material for prototypes?

In most cases PLA. For function tests needing heat or toughness, PETG or ASA.

All values are practical guide values for FDM printers with a 0.4 mm nozzle. Every printer, filament and slicer behaves a little differently: always print a small test piece for fits.
