Save money on 3D printing

The price of a print depends mostly on print time and only then on material. Here is where you can really turn the dials.

Why print time drives the price

A print costs material, power and machine time. For small and medium parts the material is often the smaller item: a 1 kg spool often costs 15 to 30 € depending on type, so a part using 50 g costs only a euro or two. The machine, however, may run six hours and wear while doing so. Anyone selling prints therefore usually bills by time. Anything that shortens print time reliably saves money.

The cost calculator shows how that looks for your part. Enter weight and time from your slicer, adjust power and material price, done.

The levers, by impact

  1. Check the size. Volume grows with the cube: a part at 80 % of the dimensions has only about half the volume (0.8³ ≈ 0.51). Does it really need to be that big?
  2. Lower the infill. For decor and models 10 to 15 % is enough. Beyond roughly 20 to 30 % many parts gain little strength but lose a lot of time and material. For loaded parts, more walls help more than more infill.
  3. Raise the layer height. Going from 0.2 to 0.28 mm means about a third fewer layers. Time often drops noticeably, though not in the same ratio for every part. Curves become slightly coarser.
  4. Change the orientation. A part lying flat and needing no supports saves material and clean-up. Try orientations with the print bed preview.
  5. Avoid supports. FDM usually prints overhangs up to about 45° without supports. Splitting a part in two and gluing it can be cheaper than large support structures.
  6. Larger nozzle for large parts. A 0.6 mm nozzle can cut time clearly on coarse parts, at the cost of detail.
  7. Several parts per plate. Printing several parts in one run saves heat-up and swap time.
  8. Choose a fitting material. PLA is usually cheapest and easiest. Use PETG, ABS or ASA only when heat, toughness or UV demand it. Fibre grades cost more and need a hardened nozzle (materials).

When printing yourself pays off, and when not

For one-offs, spare parts and prototypes your own printer is usually unbeatable because there are no set-up costs. For large quantities, very fine detail or special materials a service or another process can be cheaper. Calculate both before deciding.

Frequently asked questions

What affects the price of a 3D print most?

Usually print time. Material is often the smaller item for small and medium parts. Layer height, infill, size and supports determine the time.

How much infill do I need?

For decor and models 10 to 15 % is usually enough. For loaded parts, extra walls are often more effective than more infill.

Does a thicker layer height really save money?

Yes, print time drops because fewer layers are needed. The surface becomes a little coarser, and the saving is smaller than the layer-height ratio for most parts.

Where do I calculate my print?

In the 3DToolBox cost calculator, right in the browser and without sign-up.
